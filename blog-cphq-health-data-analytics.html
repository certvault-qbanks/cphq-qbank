      Types of measures

      Donabedian's framework sorts measures into structure (capacity to deliver care), process (what was done) and outcome (what happened to the patient). Improvement teams add balancing measures, which watch for unintended harm elsewhere, like readmissions rising after you shorten length of stay.

      Exam trap

      If a rate suddenly improves, check whether the definition, exclusions or data source changed before celebrating.

      Sampling methods

      • Simple random: every case has an equal chance of selection.
      • Systematic: every nth case from a list, with a random start.
      • Stratified: random samples from subgroups (units, payers, age groups) so each is represented.
      • Convenience: whatever is easy to get. Fast, but prone to bias.

      Descriptive statistics

      Mean, median and mode describe the center of your data; standard deviation and range describe spread. For skewed data like length of stay, where a few long stays pull the average up, the median is the better summary. Paste some numbers to see the difference.

      Which statistical test?

      ToolUse it toExample
      t-testCompare the means of two groupsAverage wait time before vs. after a change
      ANOVACompare means of three or more groupsAverage LOS across four units
      Chi-squareTest association between categorical variablesReadmission (yes/no) by discharge disposition
      Correlation (r)Measure strength and direction of a linear relationship, from −1 to +1Nurse staffing and fall rates
      RegressionPredict an outcome from one or more variablesPredicting readmission risk

      Remember: correlation is not causation, and a p-value below 0.05 is the conventional threshold for statistical significance.

      Statistical process control

      Every process varies. Common cause variation is the natural, random noise built into a stable process; reacting to it (tampering) makes things worse. Special cause variation comes from something unusual and should be investigated. Control charts separate the two with a centerline and upper and lower control limits, typically set at three standard deviations.

      Spot the signalPick a scenario

      Common special cause rules

      • A single point outside a control limit.
      • A shift: a run of consecutive points on one side of the centerline (8 is common; some rule sets use 7 or 9).
      • A trend: 6 or more consecutive points steadily rising or falling.
      • Two of three consecutive points near a control limit (beyond two standard deviations, same side).

      Rule sets vary slightly by source. Know the concept behind each rule rather than a single number.

      Dashboards, scorecards and benchmarks

      A dashboard shows current performance on a few key measures, often with status colors, for quick monitoring. A scorecard tracks performance against targets over a longer period and is often tied to strategy. Tailor both to the audience: boards need a handful of strategic measures, while unit managers need operational detail.

      Benchmarking compares your performance with an external reference, such as national databases or high-performing peers. Make sure the comparison group uses the same definitions and is risk-adjusted where appropriate.

      Practice questionHealth Data Analytics

      A unit manager sees one month's hand hygiene compliance fall from 91% to 86%. The control chart shows the point is within limits and no other rule is triggered. What is the best response?

      With no special cause signal, the drop is consistent with common cause variation. Reacting to it as if it were special (tampering) wastes resources and can increase variation. If overall performance is below goal, improving the system as a whole is a separate decision.

      Practice questionHealth Data Analytics

      A hospital wants to track falls per 1,000 patient days. Patient days differ each month, and a single patient can fall more than once. Which control chart should be used?

      Falls are counts of events (defects), a patient can have more than one, and the area of opportunity (patient days) varies. That combination calls for a u-chart. A c-chart assumes a constant area of opportunity; p- and np-charts are for yes/no "defective" data.
